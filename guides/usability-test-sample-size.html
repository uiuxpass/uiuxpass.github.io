How many users do you need for a usability test? Honest numbers

6 min read · Updated 23 November 2026

"Test with five users" is good advice for one job and bad advice for another. Five users are enough to find most usability problems. They are nowhere near enough to measure how many people succeed. Mixing the two up is how teams ship with false confidence.

Finding problems: five users go a long way

If a problem affects 31% of users (the average in Nielsen and Landauer's classic data), the chance that at least one tester hits it grows quickly:

Testers135810
Problem affecting 31% of users31%67%84%95%98%
Problem affecting 10% of users10%27%41%57%65%

Chance of seeing the problem at least once, calculated as 1 − (1 − p)n. Rarer problems need more people, which is why several small rounds beat one big one.

Measuring success: five users tell you very little

Say 4 of 5 people completed the task. That is 80%, but the honest 95% range (Wilson interval) is 38% to 96%. Your real success rate could be worse than a coin flip.

ResultObserved95% range
3 of 560%23% – 88%

Even 5 of 5 only tells you the true rate is probably above 57%. To report a success rate a stakeholder can act on, you need 20 or more people, or you report the range, not the single number.

What to do instead

  • Exploring a design? 5 people per round, fix, repeat. Report problems with how many testers hit them, not percentages.
  • Comparing two designs or proving a target? Plan for 20+ per design, or use an unmoderated test to get the numbers cheaply.
  • Always report the range. "80% (95% range 38–96%, n = 5)" is honest and stops the meeting from treating it as fact.
